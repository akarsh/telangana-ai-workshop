# Lab 4 explanation — why takeaway titles change how a deck works

## Topic titles vs. takeaway titles

"District Breakdown" tells the audience what the slide is *about*. "Mahabubnagar and Nizamabad have the highest beginner share and the largest device gaps" tells them what the slide *means*. The test the workshop slides use is: can someone follow the argument by reading only the five titles, without seeing a single chart? A topic title fails that test — you'd have to actually look at the slide to learn anything. A takeaway title passes it, which is also why it works as a spoken headline when presenting.

## Why the story is built before any slide is designed

If you open a slide editor first, the natural instinct is to put the data on the slide and figure out what it means later — which produces a slide that shows a chart but doesn't say anything. Filling in the story-arc table first forces the meaning to exist as a sentence before it exists as a slide, so the design step becomes "how do I support this sentence visually" instead of "what do I do with this data."

## Why slide 3 ("meaning") is the one people skip

Under time pressure, it's tempting to go straight from "here's the data" (slide 2) to "here's what we should do" (slide 4) — but that skips explaining *why* the data implies that action. A reader who disagrees with the recommendation and can't find the reasoning behind it will either reject it or ask a question the deck should have already answered. Slide 3 is what makes slide 4 arguable rather than just asserted.

## Why the close asks for one specific thing

"What happens next?" answered with "any questions?" leaves leadership unclear on what agreeing even means. Naming one action with a deadline (two extra facilitators, by Thursday) gives them something concrete to approve or push back on — vague asks get vague responses.
